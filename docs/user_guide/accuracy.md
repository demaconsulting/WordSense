# Accuracy and Model Choice

This chapter explains what the published accuracy figures of the three models measure, how to read the model table that
follows it, how to choose a model, and which caveats apply to the figures. The figures themselves are in the model
table (the next chapter, generated by the builder and equal to the table of the README).

## What the Models Are Measured On

Every figure is measured through the public model interface on data that no weight, threshold or hyperparameter has
seen. The sets are:

- **Real-text sense.** The test half of the real held-out sentences of the corpus (2,397 sentences of Project Gutenberg
  books, VOA, USGS, NPS and National Weather Service text, and Tatoeba sentences under CC0). The corpus splits the real
  held-out sentences by document: one half calibrates the thresholds of each lemma, the other half is never used for
  anything but this measurement. The sentences were found or written for specific senses, so the set is balanced by
  sense: it measures the classifier, not the share of each sense in running text. The easier, written held-out
  sentences are reported by the builder as a secondary set and are not in the table. Every real held-out sentence was
  labeled again by a blind labeler that never saw the gold label, and the 574 rows that were not agreed were judged by a
  second blind judge; 166 rows were relabeled and 87 removed before the figures were measured. The same check was then
  run on the 20,059 real anchor (training) rows: 1,709 rows were not agreed and judged by the second judge, 469 rows
  were relabeled and 185 removed. The 469 relabels were then judged again, blind, by two models of different families
  (claude-sonnet-5.5 and gpt-6.1-sol); the 225 that both confirmed were kept and the other 244 were reverted. The models
  were retrained on the corrected anchors.
- **Running-text part of speech.** The part of speech of the selected analysis on the test split of the representative
  open corpus (1,501 sentences, 21,482 scored tokens of random CC0 text), on the English EWT treebank test file mapped to
  the conventions of the dictionary (19,468 scored tokens; the treebank is downloaded for evaluation only and is never
  committed; a possessive of a numeral such as `80's` is not answered, because numerals are unknown) and on the 32
  sentences of the simplified-technical-English challenge set (reported on its own: it is small
  and chosen for its hard cases, so read it as a regression check). "Ambiguous" counts only the tokens whose word has
  two or more parts of speech in the dictionary.
- **Independent sense evaluation (SemCor).** The 31,556 single-word annotations of the 825 trained lemmas that SemCor 3.0
  can evaluate (the sense-annotated Brown Corpus, with WordNet 3.0 sense keys mapped to the senses of the catalog; 97.1%
  of the keys map). SemCor is downloaded to a cache by the builder and never committed. The table gives the share of
  words answered at the sense level, the precision of those answers and the *fine top-1*, the share of words whose most
  probable sense is right. The table covers the trained lemmas only; "SemCor Beyond the Trained Lemmas" below measures
  all mapped words of a trained lemma or of an untrained lemma with two or more senses.
- **The reviewed suite.** 348 hand-checked sentences about common ambiguous words guard each model against
  regressions in the test suite of the repository. They are a regression check, not an accuracy measure.

## Reading the Model Table

The accuracy columns are deterministic for a given build, up to the CPU effect described below. The size, memory,
latency and first-use columns depend on the machine. They were measured with the builder in the Release configuration on
.NET 10 with ONNX Runtime on the CPU (two intra-op threads), one sentence at a time, on an Intel Core i7-13850HX laptop
(20 cores, 64 GB RAM, Windows 11) that was otherwise idle. Repeated runs agreed within 1 percent in latency, within 3
percent in the model part of the first use and within 2 MiB in memory. Another machine gives other values: read them as
an order of magnitude and as the ratio between the models, not as a guarantee. The download column is the sum of the
pinned files of the model in MiB (MiniLM 22.1 MiB, BertBaseCased 104.3 MiB).

## Choosing a Model

The part of speech is the dependable part of an answer, and it is about the same quality in all three models (93.8 to
94.1% of the tokens of running text in the open corpus test split), because the Basic tagger sets the part of speech of
most running words. Choose by what else you need:

- **Basic** (core package, no download, about 0.1 ms a sentence) answers the part of speech of every word and the sense
  of a word that has one. Use it when the part of speech is all you need, when nothing may be downloaded, or as the
  fallback of the other two.
- **BertBaseCased** (104 MiB, about 8 ms a sentence) is the model for fine senses. It costs about five times the
  latency and the download of MiniLM, and in return it commits to a fine sense on 27.2% of the real test sentences and
  is right in 94.5% of those answers; its most probable sense is the best of the three (72.0% of the real test
  sentences, 63.7% of the SemCor words).
- **MiniLM** (22 MiB, about 1.5 ms a sentence) answers mostly at the part of speech or the group level: it commits to a
  fine sense on 7.9% of the real test sentences (96.8% right). Choose it for the small download and the speed, not for
  senses. It is the default recommendation when a download is acceptable and the part of speech and groups are enough.

Whichever model you choose, test the `Level` of every answer first (see "Levels and Confidence"), treat the sense level
as a candidate to check and the part of speech as the dependable part, and tune any threshold on your own text.

### Accuracy of the Basic Model

Basic is a second-order hidden Markov model over thirteen tags, fused with naive Bayes context cues for the lemmas that
have at least two senses. The tag statistics are trained from the open part-of-speech corpus of this project (CC0), not
from a treebank. On running text it reaches 93.87% of all tokens (91.53% of the ambiguous tokens) on the open corpus
test split and 91.65% (88.62%) on the mapped treebank test file; on the technical challenge set it reaches 98.65%. The
open test split shares annotators and genres with the training split, so its figure is optimistic; the treebank figure
is the cross-domain one. Basic limits to keep in mind:

- It chooses among the senses of the dictionary; a word that is not in the dictionary has no answer, and a tokenizer
  that splits a contraction such as "don't" differently from the dictionary loses the word.
- It never abstains and never names a fine sense or a group for an analysis that has several senses. A heteronym with
  two meanings of the same part of speech is decided only for the lemmas that have a cue table.
- Its confidence is a ranking signal, not a guarantee: it is roughly calibrated at 0.9 and above and over-confident
  below 0.6.
- It reads the case of a word that has both a capitalized and a lower-case lemma that the lower-case spelling carries
  (`Polish` and `polish`, `August` and `august`): a lower-case word, or an isolated capitalized word inside a sentence,
  weighs the analyses of the other case down to a tenth. A sentence-initial capital, a capitalized word next to another
  capitalized word (`State Department`), an upper-case or mixed-case word and the words of a title-case segment carry
  no evidence. This is a prior on the
  writer, not a rule. A lemma that only the exact capitalized spelling has (`State`, `May`, `Jack`) is not a candidate:
  looking the capitalized spelling up as written made Basic answer the capitalized lemma for common words that legal and
  formal text capitalizes (`each State`, `any State`), about 70 SemCor words that it then answered wrongly at the sense
  level, so Basic keeps the lower-case lemma for them. On the reviewed suite the rule fixed the `Polish`, `polish` and
  `august` rows (see `corpus/suite/README.md`).

## The Usage Prior and the SemCor Caveat

The encoder classifiers are trained on sentences that are balanced by sense (about 20 sentences for a frequent sense,
5 to 10 for a rare one), so their raw probabilities carry the proportions of the training data, not those of real use,
and rare senses are over-predicted. The builder corrects this when it trains the weights: it divides each sense score by
the share of the sense among the training sentences of its lemma and multiplies in the usage of the sense, taken from
the WordNet 3.0 tag counts, with smoothing and tempered by the exponent 0.5 (because the counts are small and come from
the Brown Corpus, a different genre). The correction has no run-time cost. When it was introduced (measured on the
corpus of that time), the effect on the shipped models was:

- On SemCor the fine top-1 rose from 47.5% to 53.6% (MiniLM) and from 56.9% to 60.7% (BertBaseCased), and the share of
  sense-level answers and their precision rose too.
- On the real test sentences, which are balanced by sense and therefore reward the old proportions, the fine top-1 moved
  by +0.05 (MiniLM) and -1.15 points (BertBaseCased), and the running-text part of speech did not fall.
- Re-weighting the real test sentences by an independent sense prior (the share of each sense among the randomly
  harvested candidate sentences) raised the fine top-1 by 2.6 (MiniLM) and 1.8 points (BertBaseCased), so the gain is not
  an artifact of the WordNet counts.

The current figures are in the model table (fine top-1 on SemCor: 56.4% for MiniLM and 63.7% for BertBaseCased).

Two caveats apply. First, **SemCor is circular for this prior**: the WordNet 3.0 counts include the counts of SemCor
itself, so part of the gain on SemCor is the prior having seen the answers. The same holds for the first-sense
baseline of the evaluation (60.7% of the SemCor words, always choosing the first sense of the right lemma and part of
speech in Open English WordNet order): that order follows Princeton WordNet's frequency order (it matches the WordNet
3.0 top-count sense in 10,047 of the 10,062 blocks with a unique maximum), and those counts include the tags of
SemCor, so the baseline is partly circular and optimistic. That is why the exponent is tempered; a larger exponent
raises the SemCor figure further but lowers the share of sense-level
answers and the part of speech on the balanced real test sentences. Second, the prior is per lemma and sense, not per
context, and a sense with very few training sentences is lifted the most by the correction, so the correction is more
faithful as the per-sense quotas of the corpus are filled. The sense choice of BertBaseCased is a little better than the
first-sense baseline for the words it covers (63.7% against 60.7%, an optimistic baseline) and that of MiniLM is not
(56.4%).

## SemCor Beyond the Trained Lemmas

The SemCor cell of the model table describes the 31,556 words of the 825 trained lemmas that SemCor can evaluate. Most
ambiguous words of running text are not of a trained lemma, so the builder (`evaluate-senses`) also measures the models
on **all words**: every SemCor single-word annotation whose key maps to a sense of the catalog and whose lemma is
trained or has two or more senses (counted over all parts of speech). Of the 203,959 mapped words, 31,556 are of a
trained lemma, 152,767 are of an untrained lemma with two or more senses and 19,636 are of an untrained lemma with fewer
than two senses, which are left out because every model answers them at the sense level. Of the 184,323 words of the
all-words set, 17.1% are of a trained lemma. The trained rows below are the figures of the model table.

| Model | Words | Sense answered | Sense precision | Fine top-1 |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Basic | trained (31,556) | 3.25% | 68.91% | 2.53% |
| Basic | untrained (152,767) | 8.75% | 40.00% | 4.09% |
| Basic | all (184,323) | 7.81% | 42.06% | 3.82% |
| MiniLM | trained | 8.33% | 88.71% | 56.41% |
| MiniLM | untrained | 8.74% | 40.00% | 4.07% |
| MiniLM | all | 8.67% | 48.01% | 13.03% |
| BertBaseCased | trained | 26.87% | 86.63% | 63.69% |
| BertBaseCased | untrained | 8.77% | 39.90% | 4.08% |
| BertBaseCased | all | 11.87% | 58.01% | 14.28% |
| First sense with the highest WordNet 3.0 tag count | trained | | | 60.76% |
| First sense with the highest WordNet 3.0 tag count | untrained | | | 71.53% |
| First sense with the highest WordNet 3.0 tag count | all | | | 69.68% |

What the figures say:

- For a lemma that a model was not trained for, none of the three models ranks the senses: a word with one analysis and
  one sense is answered at the sense level, every other word at the part-of-speech level without a most probable sense,
  and a word without a most probable sense counts as a miss of the fine top-1. That is why the untrained fine top-1 is
  4% for all three models, and it is almost the same for the three because it comes mainly from the lemmas whose part
  of speech has one sense (the share of the sense answers, about 9%, is also the same). The sense precision of those
  answers, 40%, is low mainly because the sense is right only when the part of speech that Basic chose for the word is
  right as well.
- Over all words, the most probable sense of BertBaseCased is right for 14.3% of the words, of MiniLM for 13.0% and of Basic
  for 3.8%. The gain of BertBaseCased over the other models is on the 17.1% of the words of trained lemmas.
- **The baseline is circular.** The first sense with the highest WordNet 3.0 tag count is right for 69.7% of all words,
  but the tag counts are counted on SemCor itself, so on SemCor the baseline has seen the answers: read it as an
  optimistic reference and not as a competitor. It is also a prior per lemma that the library does not ship for the
  lemmas it was not trained for (the models carry no prior at all for them); the gap to the baseline on the untrained
  words therefore shows what such a prior could add, not a defect of the evaluation. On the trained words the tag-count
  baseline (60.76%) agrees with the first-sense baseline in inventory order (60.75%) to a few hundredths of a point.
- The all-words set leaves out the untrained lemmas with fewer than two senses and the 2.9% of the single-key words whose
  key does not map to a sense of the catalog; the figures say nothing about them.

## The Calibration Target and the Share of Sense Answers

The builder chooses the thresholds of a lemma by cross-validation on its anchors (precision target 95%) and then raises
them with one pooled shift so that the answers reach the real-text calibration target (0.97 per lemma, two points above
the 95% because the whole pipeline is about two points less precise than the classifier alone) on the real calibration
half of the held-out sentences. The shift is the lowest one at which the pooled calibration answers reach the target
while at least 30% of those answers are kept (and at least 30 answers). If no such shift exists, the target is reported
as "not reached" and the shift is the largest one that keeps 30% of the answers; the group thresholds follow the same
rule,
and the lemmas with at least 20 calibration rows also get a threshold of their own that can only raise theirs. The 30%
share is the robustness choice: a rule that accepted the first crossing of the target in the far tail (a few dozen
answers) let one wrong row move the shift by several units, so the share of sense answers of BertBaseCased changed
several-fold with a few confident errors in the calibration sentences. Measured on the calibration sentences only (before
the anchor check and the later quota waves, and not remeasured since; the test sentences were not used to design the
rule), the
shift of BertBaseCased moves between 4.66 and 5.00 over 200 bootstrap resamples with the share (4.85 to 8.15 without
it), and removing the eight most confident wrong rows moves it by 0.03 (3.2 without the share); for MiniLM the
figures are 3.56 to 4.03 against 4.07 to 6.60. On the current corpus the target 0.97 is reached for BertBaseCased
(shift 3.00: 608 of the 1,314 sense answers of the calibration sentences are kept, at a precision of 97.5%) and is not
reached for MiniLM (shift 3.14: 163 of 627 answers are kept, at 95.7%), so the thresholds of MiniLM keep 30% of the
pooled calibration answers. A target that the calibration can reach, such as 0.90, is reached by both models.
For comparison only, the figures on the real test sentences (share @ precision) for 0.90 and for the shipped 0.97:

| Model | Target 0.97 (shipped; reached by BertBaseCased only) | Target 0.90 (reached) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MiniLM | 7.9% @ 96.8% | 22.8% @ 88.1% |
| BertBaseCased | 27.2% @ 94.5% | 52.7% @ 88.1% |

Read the share of sense answers as a property of the calibration target, not as a measure of the classifier: the fine
top-1 (61.1% for MiniLM and 72.0% for BertBaseCased on the real test sentences) does not depend on the target.

## Variation Between CPUs

The encoder models run int8-quantized ONNX models on the CPU, and the int8 arithmetic differs slightly between CPUs and
operating systems. The confidences move a little (the tests compare them within 0.10 for the encoder models and within
0.01 for Basic, which is deterministic managed code), and a word whose margin lies close to its threshold can be answered
one level more or less precisely: BertBaseCased answers `back` at the sense level on one CPU and at its group
`back#group:return` on another. The figures of the model table were measured on one machine; on another CPU the accuracy
columns can differ in the last digits.
